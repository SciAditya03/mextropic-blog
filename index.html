Closing the loop: why a model gets better only if its lab data comes back

A model does not get better simply because it makes predictions. It gets better when reality answers back.

Authors · date · reading time: not supplied

Contents

Summary

Predictions become useful when measurements come back: linked to the right candidate, quality-checked, and used to guide the next decision. Published campaigns show this can improve specific outcomes, but not every round and not automatically.

In a 2023 study, researchers used Bayesian optimization to find aluminum-based catalysts that control the stereochemistry of a polymerization reaction. The model proposed candidates, chemists synthesized and tested 33 catalysts, and the measurements went back into the training data for the next round [1].

The mean absolute error between predicted and measured stereoselectivity fell from 0.36 in the first round to 0.10 in the third, and later rounds identified a greater proportion of high-performing catalysts. That is closed-loop learning: predict, experiment, capture the results, update the model, and choose what to test next.

For AI-driven drug discovery and protein engineering, the challenge is rarely generating more candidates. It is making sure results, including failures and uncertain outcomes, return to the modelling workflow in a structured, usable form.

01What the loop actually is

A prediction is a model’s estimate for an untested molecule, protein, or condition. Experimental validation tests that estimate under defined conditions. Model updating is the separate step in which the measurement enters the data used to refine the model or guide future experiments. Skipping that last step is what separates open-loop from closed-loop discovery: the laboratory tests what the model proposes, but the results are never systematically connected back.

Bayesian optimization is one way to close the loop: a surrogate model learns from accumulated observations while an acquisition function picks the next experiment [2][3]. Self-driving laboratories extend this with automated experiments and characterization [2]. But a loop is defined by a repeated process, not by robots or a particular algorithm. Experiment selection can use active learning, Bayesian optimization, simpler heuristics, or expert judgment, and the update can be retraining, recalibration, or revised selection rules.

Two stages are easy to underestimate. Identity tracking gives every candidate a stable identifier that links its design to the physical sample, raw data, and final measurement. Quality control separates unreliable measurements and failed builds from genuine outcomes. Without them, results can be assigned to the wrong design or become unusable for model updating.

02What makes a result learnable

Feedback is useful only if the model can answer three questions about every result: which candidate was tested, what was measured, and under what conditions?

Traceability starts with persistent identifiers; the FAIR principles stress identifiers, rich metadata, and provenance so data can be found, interpreted, and reused [4]. Without that link, a measurement may exist, but the model cannot connect it to the prediction it was meant to test. Metadata supplies the context: “42% inhibition” is incomplete without the assay, target, concentration, controls, replicates, and processing method.

Failures must be recorded accurately. A construct that fails to express is not equivalent to one that expresses but shows no activity, and a failed quality-control check is not a genuine negative result. Classified separately, they stop the model from learning the wrong lesson.

Laboratory variation matters too. Differences in reagent lots, instruments, personnel, and batches can introduce systematic effects that resemble biological signal, particularly when they correlate with the outcome [5]. Assay interference can also produce misleading hits [6]. And because active-learning systems choose what to test, selection decisions must be documented to avoid misleading estimates of performance [3].

Practical principleMetadata does not make experimental data perfect; it makes the data interpretable, traceable, and auditable.

03What real campaigns show

Closed-loop learning has been demonstrated across protein engineering, drug discovery, materials science, and catalysis. The table summarises four published campaigns, each of which should be read within its own domain, baseline, and design.

The results are not uniformly positive, which is the point. In the CXCR4–CXCL12 campaign, the second screening round produced weak or no activity before six of ten compounds in the third round showed strong readouts [8]. In the polymer nanoparticle laboratory, model R² rose from 0.889 to 0.946 after the first feedback generation, then declined to 0.905 as the selected formulations grew more complex [9]. That is a useful counterexample to the assumption that every feedback cycle improves performance.

Together, these studies show that feedback can guide decisions and improve specific outcomes. They do not show that it invariably improves accuracy, or that the same strategy will work equally well across all biological systems.

04An opportunity, not a guarantee

Closed-loop learning can make discovery more efficient, but it does not remove the difficulty of modelling biology. Six failure modes deserve attention:

  • Noisy labels. Assay variability and instrument drift can mislead uncertainty-based selection toward examples that are noisy rather than informative.
  • Batch effects. A model may learn technical artefacts instead of biology. Replication, controls, randomization, and recorded covariates reduce the risk [5].
  • Distribution shift. A model trained on one cell type, laboratory, or assay may perform poorly in another, so independent validation matters.
  • Biased selection. Testing only candidates predicted to succeed leaves the rest of the design space unexplored.
  • Data leakage. Related samples in both training and evaluation sets make performance look better than it is; careful splitting is essential [10].
  • More data is not always better. New measurements can be redundant, noisy, or poorly matched to the objective.

It also helps to evaluate two outcomes separately. Predictive performance asks whether the model is more accurate on held-out data, using metrics such as mean absolute error, ranking, and calibration. Campaign efficiency asks whether the workflow finds better candidates with fewer experiments, less time, or less material. A model can become more accurate overall without improving the campaign if its remaining errors fall among the candidates that matter most. The strongest evidence compares outcomes against a defined baseline under the same budget; retrospective simulations can support a hypothesis but do not alone establish prospective laboratory benefit.

05Where Mextropic fits

Mextropic's approach

At Mextropic, we focus on connecting computational design with experimental evidence through a Design → Build → Test → Learn workflow. Researchers drive the design and learning stages, while we support the build and test stages by returning structured, machine-learning-ready biomedical data [11].

Experimental results are most useful when they can be traced to the right candidate and interpreted in context. Our approach emphasizes data that can be linked to its source, with failures returned, measurement limits identified, and relevant experimental covariates made available. This helps research teams distinguish a genuine negative biological result from a failed construct or a quality-control issue.

Returning experimental data is an important step in closing the loop, but it is not the whole loop. How those results inform model updates, subsequent experiment selection, and performance evaluation depends on how each research team integrates and validates the data within its workflow.

Our focus is to make experimental evidence more structured, traceable, and usable for the next stage of discovery.

06Predictions become useful when experiments answer back

A prediction is a hypothesis about what may happen, not evidence that it has. A feedback loop begins to deliver value when the measurement is linked to the correct candidate, interpreted in its experimental context, and used to inform later decisions, and when the resulting changes are assessed against suitable baselines and independent data.

The aim is not to make every cycle look successful, but to learn which predictions are reliable, where uncertainty remains, and which experiment is most informative next. For AI-driven experimental biology, that means laboratories and modelling teams designing their data systems together, so every result can be traced, quality-checked, interpreted in context, and evaluated before it shapes the next round [5][10].
